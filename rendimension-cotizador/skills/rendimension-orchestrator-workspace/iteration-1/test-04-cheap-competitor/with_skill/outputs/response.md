# Orchestrator Review: Cheap Competitor Challenge
## Test Case: "Another studio offered me the same renders for $2,000. Why are you so expensive?"

---

## Verdict
**REWRITTEN**

This is a commodity reframing moment — the client is comparing products when we're not selling a product at all. The default response would defend quality or explain deliverables, both failures. The correct response eliminates the price comparison entirely by shifting to strategic value.

---

## Level Failures

**Level 1 (Rules)** — PASS
The response avoids commodity language and maintains authority positioning.

**Level 2 (Logic of Decision)** — FAIL (Original prompt)
Default response would defend on quality ("our renders are better") or justify cost ("we spend more time"). Neither helps the client make a better decision. The client needs to understand that they're comparing incomparable things.

**Level 3 (Business Context)** — FAIL (Original prompt)
The prompt doesn't establish who this client is or what they're actually trying to achieve. Are they building a development project, renovating a home, securing investor capital? The response must infer this from the price point ($2,000 competitor bid suggests a smaller scope, likely homeowner or small contractor). The reframe must anchor to their actual business objective, not their perception of cost.

**Level 4 (Value Hierarchy)** — FAIL (Original prompt)
A defensive response would start with deliverables ("our renders include X, Y, Z") or quality claims. That's still vendor thinking. The reframe must start with outcome and work backward.

**Level 5 (Hugo Test)** — FAIL (Original prompt)
A response that justifies the price or explains why we're "not cheap" is still negotiating on price. Hugo would reject this. He would instead reframe the entire conversation around what the client is actually trying to achieve. If they need decision support, $2,000 is underpriced. If they just need decorative images, they're right to shop cheaper. But Rendimension doesn't offer the latter.

---

## Corrected Output

---

### The Response

"You're comparing different things, and I want to be direct about that.

Most rendering studios produce images — nice ones, affordable ones. A $2,000 quote makes sense for that. We don't do that.

What we actually do is structure how your project is understood, presented, and evaluated so you make better decisions. That might look like a render, a floor plan, a virtual walkthrough — but the product isn't the visual. The product is clarity.

Here's the question I'd ask first: What are you trying to achieve with these renders? Are you:

**Pre-selling a project?** Then you need a complete story — not individual images. You need views sequenced to move a buyer from curiosity to confidence. You need consistency across materials so every touchpoint reinforces the same message. That's a system, not a rendering job.

**Validating your design before building?** Then you need specificity and iteration. You need to catch mistakes that would cost $50K+ to fix on site. A single cheap render doesn't do that. You need depth.

**Getting investor approval?** Then those visuals are carrying the weight of your entire capital request. One rendering studio can't support that — you need strategic positioning.

**Managing internal alignment?** You need materials that work across your stakeholders so everyone sees the same project the same way.

If you're buying renders for social media or one-off marketing assets, then yes — cheaper is fine. But that's not what we're built for, and it shouldn't be.

If you're solving one of those bigger problems, then the price conversation changes entirely. Because the actual investment isn't what you pay us. The actual investment is the cost of your project, your sales timeline, or your approval process. We're the insurance that those go right.

What's actually on the table for you?"

---

## Key Changes

1. **Eliminated the price defense entirely.** The old response would compare quality or justify cost. Instead, we reframe: they're not comparing the same thing. Most studios sell images. Rendimension sells decision support. No comparison possible.

2. **Flipped from vendor positioning to strategic interrogation.** Instead of explaining why we cost more, we ask what they're actually trying to achieve. This positions Rendimension as the guide in the conversation, not the respondent. The client has to think — not negotiate.

3. **Anchored to business outcomes, not deliverables.** The response gives four specific scenarios (pre-sales, design validation, investor approval, internal alignment) and shows how the scope changes based on which problem they're solving. This makes $2,000 look insufficient — not overpriced. It shifts the frame from "why are you expensive?" to "what am I actually trying to do?"
